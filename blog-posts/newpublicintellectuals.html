An Outsiders Impression of the New Public Intellectuals

I have recently been involved in the humanities for the first time in an academic setting. Academics are not the subject of these reflections; that is, unless they are also part of the almost-entirely-online ‘new public intellectualism’. I am bringing it up to point to an important distinction noted by Baert and Shipman1, who recognised the two things are quite different. The influence of the academy works indirectly, competes for students in terms of the employability of qualifications, and is constrained by institutional logistics as much as by institutional oversight. Though some may not like to admit it, academics are not just educators, but are also labourers of a more traditional sort. The privilege of tenure is limited by the number of academic posts, and academics are therefore those who fit the demands of a traditional labour market. To be sanction to teach a higher education requires being satisfied with a particular salary, being accountable to an employer, being willing and able to receive a certain amount of mandatory training, being willing to move to a particular location, being able to speak a particular language, etc. Even having the ability to meet the professional standards that would not cause a scandal is a significant, if uncontroversial, constraint on who receives academic tenure. The world of public intellectuals is quite different. There is little-to-no oversight, no requirement to move, no mandatory training, no upper limit on a days’ wage. Access to an audience is not limited by class sizes but search engine optimisation and trends. The contest for access is fought within an attention economy rather than an institutional one.

There are obviously overlaps, and an academic reputation certainly converts to ‘clout’ within the public sphere. It is maybe best to understand a career as an ‘online-intellectual’ as academy-adjacent; an alternative or extra-curricular career path rather than a fully-fledged resistance or opposition; the institutional career does not sufficiently remunerate those who take it on, nor does it provide the maximally visible platform from which to speak.

With the stage set, the technology in place, the barriers between producer and consumer removed, a market for intellectual discourse now thrives. Those with the means can and do enjoy an unprecedented freedom and democracy to educate and be educated. The freedom is, in this sense, a broader reach, autonomy over content, a democratisation of voices. It is not unlike the structural ‘reset’ which occurred for the inhabitants of the New World; like the Jamestown colonists, intellectuals are able to finally govern themselves, without necessarily overthrowing their predecessors. Though that did eventually happen in the Americas, it was not immediate: a great deal happened in the 150 year period between finding their freedom and asserting their complete independence—much more than someone only loosely acquainted with the facts might be aware of. The parallels between these two situations may prove to have more in common than just vague analogy as time goes on.

Much can be learned about the American project and its evolution from consideration of these early settlers, their ambitions and ideas with regards to the opportunity they happened upon, beyond just the geopolitics of a terra nova and the rapid wealth they generated. These colonists arrived with a highly idiosyncratic toolkit of ideas, and founded a highly idiosyncratic culture as a result. I suggest we take the hint from history, and take a good, hard anthropological look at the terra nova which public intellectuals occupy, and we do it now, because if the American project is even partially accurate, thing are going to change rapidly and irreversibly.

What seems pertinent at this time is what has not been said. Thanks to the perennial lust for novelty, there has been a deluge of conversation about what is new/different—about the dangers of an algorithmically driven attention economy on the one hand while a democratisation of knowledge flourishes on the other—but a complete lack of commentary on what has been preserved. The survival of an almost entirely intact intellectual sensibility in this new domain is quite remarkable, when you really think about it. It seems as though these pioneering intellectuals are entirely satisfied with the institutional norms regarding education, and the values they betray—discipline, rigour, eloquent presentation, a high regard for the text and for the discursive mode, an insistence on ‘recognition’ in the form of citations, a preference for brevity and esoteric technical jargon, encouragement for exhaustive treatment of niche topics. It will be obvious to anyone that I myself respect these values—at least enough to employ them myself—but that does not invalidate my interest in their effects. What does a student sacrifice when institutional norms are internalised as a moral law or modus operandi? What is produced when they become the embedded in a network, and how does such a network behave?

In scholarship, the word is sovereign. Even in programmes where some level of physical/craft skill is expected, the discursive dimensions of the subject are what is being judged. My own qualification as a ‘Bachelor of Science’ in navigation demonstrates this quite well, with not a single part of the grading criteria being the physical act of navigating a vessel on the water. The prevalence of ‘-ology’ within scholarship is a telling trend regarding this, being derived from the greek λόγος, ‘word’. Not only does this trend suggest the Hellenistic—Platonic, to be precise—roots of academic scholarship, but the character and orientation of the institution itself. The taboos of such an institution are not so much the subjects to be studied or the consequences of doing so, but about improper attitudes toward knowledge itself. One does not fall from scholarly favour by choosing a taboo subject, but by ignoring a normative attitude. This attitude—what I will call logocentrism2—is not here being denounced. It is not a superstitious faith, or a tyrannical horror. That said, by its sheer existence it necessarily excludes something, and creates a particular logic as a consequence.

It would be possible to argue all day about the legitimacy of logocentrism as the pervasive force in academia, but this is not my battle to be won. My only wish is identify the character of the academic sphere, to give it a face, to sketch its contours. I am sure there is on-going work to reform the system and reclaim the centre, but at time of writing, it is hard to deny that in most cases the academy is firmly routed in this discursive prejudice.

And the reason this is so important for an understanding of the rise in public intellectuals is due to the nature of the internet; a bodiless world of logocentric substance, its very ground is not body, but a language. A human being learns to walk before they learn a single word, and could become proficient in any number of domains without ever being able to speak more than a few words. The internet, though, requires a linguistic grammar before it can even exist; its spaces can only be navigated by means of words. Its locations are disclosed by ‘searching’ a landscape of names, and its ‘sites’ are entirely media transported by a code.

Such a space was naturally bound to promote the logocentrism of intellectuals, and its invention was not possible without a sophisticated logocentrism in the first place. So far, the evidence speaks for itself: as intellectuals ‘carve out their niche’ in this terra nova, their cash crops are, not surprisingly, logocentric ones. From academic seeds, modern public intellectuals have cultivated a GMO variety of ‘lecturing’; cheaper to produce, an extended shelf-life, better value for money. Like all GMO’s, it is worth wondering what the ramifications are for the intellectual ecology.

I say the above with some exceptions. One such lecture I listened to only today. Though there was no lack of material— lecturer introduced their life's work, gave a strong account of their soteriological and metaphysical positions, and some other valuable gleanings—and yet within the hour, things wound down to a natural end. There are other examples I can point to which follow a similar trajectory, and seem to be of a different sort—logophillic, but not necessarily logocentric. It may be worth noting that a significant portion of the lecture was about ceremonial magick; does this type of intellectual-but-embodied pursuit attenuate the logocentric impulse? It is not something I can answer, but seems likely.

The more conspicuous development in the new intellectual sphere is something quite different; the formidable long-form video essay. These consist of countless hours of talking at a sustained pace as though no end is in sight. To be fair, they are not a completely novel invention, and have their antecedents in the deep history of text. One only needs look at the history of English printed books for a moment to see that for as long as there has been textual media, there has been someone willing to articulate their lofty opinions ad nauseum, and then print a completely unedited, unredacted wall of text with their name on it.

In the 16th century, fortunately for us there were significant limitations on the amount of attention one person could seek. Printing a book takes long enough in the digital age, never mind in days gone by, when each page was carefully typeset, and the volumes were bound by hand. What is truly new to the information age is the removal of these barriers to publication. What benefits these circumstances offer in democratisation are lost through sheer oversaturation. If one in every hundred-thousand videos uploaded to Youtube was a video essay or lecture—a very conservative estimate—then based on Youtube’s data, there is already enough material for five hundred years of continuous, 24 hour-a-day viewing. This estimate may actually be unrealistically low, and the total could quite realistically be about ten times that amount; 5,000 years of continuous viewing. The luxury of this amount of choice is not without cost, and the infrastructure required to enable it costs billions of dollars per year, not to mention the environmental impacts. But this is all an aside.

If I can point out a slightly unfair stereotype I find amongst the maximally logocentric, extra-extra-long-form intellectuals, it is in a certain cadence and tone of speech. It is fairly common for them to talk with a certain intensity, as though their words have weight and not in the poetic sense. The speech occurs under a pressure as though the words are felt like a literal weight, and speaking them is a form of labour which requires a whole body effort. This perceptibly somatic reaction which occurs is authentic to logocentrism, an orientation to the word as a form of work; it is not merely a momentary vibration in time and space, which begins to weaken almost instantly before dissipating into the ether, but an agent and a ‘building block’ in a quite literal way.

Of course, words nor information are completely pointless, but until someone does something with them, they might as well be, and particularly so regarding intellectual speech, since if the word really is the fulcrum around which all progress emerges—one of the more grandiose logocentric theses—then eventually someone should stop talking and start to do something. But the impulses aroused by the new public intellectual sphere seem to be mostly recursive.

There are two more motifs which I would like to point out. The first, is the amount of repetition hidden in these essays. This seems to be another residue of the academic sphere, but a much less rational one; though we all, as students, must include redundant references and summaries of the influential figures for a given topic before discussing it, we do so as a formality, a ‘jump through the hoops’. A teacher wants to know we are familiar with the canon not because they want to repeatedly re-read it themselves, but because they are judging our competence and learning. This ‘hoop-jumping’ exercise has been preserved in the migration to the terra nova, and I believe it says something about the purpose of online discourse and the nature of the new culture; online public intellectuals are not just offering takes, but performing academic fluency as a form of entertainment. The new public intellectual is less of a craftsperson in an industry of ideas, but more of a colonialist and living advertisement for logocentrism, and in that sense, it is more conservative than some would like to admit. With such a healthy serving of embellishment, it is impossible not to suggest that this ‘content’ is as much spectacle as it is discourse or the progress of knowledge. The second motif—which is really more of a pattern in the way these essays unfold—is what I will call the ‘gravitational pull of the basics’; though the essay/lecture will be titled as though it were a treatment of some specific subject, they almost always drift away from it very quickly, and instead recite the cornerstone ideas of Hegel, or Marx, or Kant, or some other foundational theorist. I appreciate the reasons for doing so, and only point it out to show the rules this new game seems to play by.

This habit suggests that the new intellectuals are hungry for the attention of the uninitiated, and I suggest that the reasoning is that the market favours those who are willing to do the work for them. This seems like a completely fair trade; of course the audience—and therefore the market—rewards those who go the extra mile. We are, however, in the very early days of having this type of reach, and my objection here is not aimed at the principle of the competitive pressure of free markets. I am not opposed to freedom, nor the democratisation of higher learning. I do, however, have questions about the future of higher learning under these conditions; and not because I want to rescue it from some imagined danger, or steer it towards certain ends.

If Gerard is right—if desires are a product of imitation—then we always do well to take note of where the trends are headed, since we will likely be following along ourselves. As a participant in the intellectual project with one foot in both camps, I have some observations which I have articulated here. I do not benefit from the ideas industry in any material way; my stakes are low and my interest is purely recreational. The only way that these changes affect me are in my own enjoyment, and, though that means I do not feel existentially threatened by the future of discourse, it does mean I notice qualitative differences in a subtle way; with the incentives removed and the investment withheld, it is far easier to take a sober look at the state of affairs. Something has clearly started to change, and if the Americas are anything to go by, the rate of change is likely to grow exponentially from here. My desire is to keep abreast of this curve, to not be caught off-guard. I am, after all is said and done, a little vulnerable to the logocentric thesis: I respect the academic norms very much, I am aware of how they shape the trajectory of thought and in-turn, the world around us. Though i am being a little grandiose, this really is the scale of the issue at hand, and though no one can control history, I would prefer to not be all-in on a sinking ship.
